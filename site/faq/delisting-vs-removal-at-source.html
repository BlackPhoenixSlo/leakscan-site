Delisting or removal at the source: which do I need?

Usually both, in parallel. They aren't two strengths of the same thing. They ask different parties to do different jobs, and the law treats them separately: section 512(c) covers a provider storing material, and section 512(d) covers a provider linking to it, such as a search engine.

Which one first?

It depends on what you're looking at today.

The limits of each

Delisting applies only to the search engine you filed with. Google says it can only remove content from its results pages, and a partial removal may only cover searches containing your name. Bing has its own form.

Removal at the source applies only to that copy. The same file on another site stays up, and the old link can keep showing in search until someone files. That's why a stale result after a successful takedown doesn't mean the notice failed.

How to tell which you got

Open the link in a private window, then search for it. Dead page and gone from search means both are done. Live page and gone from search means delisted only. Dead page still in search means it's a job for the outdated content tool. The monthly plan's weekly report marks every link as live, cached or dead for the same reason: "removed" alone doesn't tell you which one happened.

Quick answers

Is delisting enough on its own?

Sometimes it's all you can get. It stops that search engine leading people to the page, but the page stays online, reachable by link, on social media and through other search engines.

Do I need a separate notice for each?

Yes. A notice reaches only the party you send it to, so the site, its host and each search engine are separate filings.
