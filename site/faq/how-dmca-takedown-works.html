How does a DMCA takedown work?

A DMCA takedown is a written notice telling a service provider that material it stores or links to infringes your copyright. Under US law, a provider that wants to stay protected from damages has to respond "expeditiously" to a valid notice by removing the material or disabling access to it. It's a request with legal weight, not a court order.

What a valid notice has to contain

Section 512(c)(3) lists six elements. Leave one out and the provider can treat the notice as incomplete.

  1. A signature, physical or electronic, of the copyright owner or someone authorized to act for them.
  2. The work being infringed. In practice, a link to your original post.
  3. The infringing material, with enough to find it: the exact URLs, not the site's home page.
  4. Your contact details, so the provider can reach you.
  5. A good-faith statement that the use isn't authorized by the owner, their agent or the law.
  6. An accuracy statement, and a statement under penalty of perjury that you're authorized to act for the owner.

A notice you can adapt

Subject: DMCA takedown notice

I am the copyright owner of the content below (or authorized to act
for the owner). This is a notice under 17 U.S.C. § 512(c)(3).

1. Original work: [link to your original post]
2. Infringing material to remove: [exact leak URLs, one per line]
3. My contact details: [name, email]
4. I have a good-faith belief that this use is not authorized by the
   copyright owner, its agent, or the law.
5. The information in this notice is accurate, and under penalty of
   perjury, I am the owner or authorized to act for the owner.

Signature: [full name], [date]

Who it goes to

The law addresses notices to the provider's designated agent. US providers register theirs in the Copyright Office's public directory, and many sites name a contact on a DMCA or abuse page. If the site doesn't respond, the notice moves up to its host and registrar: that route is here. Google is a separate filing with its own form: see the Google guide.

What happens after you file

The provider removes the material or disables access, and has to take reasonable steps to tell the person who posted it. That person can send a counter-notice. If they do, the provider passes it to you, and puts the material back 10 to 14 business days later unless you've filed a court action. Most of the timing questions come down to this: see how long a takedown takes.

Why accuracy matters

Anyone who knowingly and materially misrepresents that something infringes can be liable for damages, costs and attorneys' fees, and Google warns about this too. It's also why I don't file in bulk. One wrong notice can get your accounts flagged, and automated tools can hit your own profile, a page you licensed or someone who just looks like you. Software finds the links; I open every one and confirm it's really yours before anything goes out.

Quick answers

Do I need a lawyer to send a DMCA notice?

No. The statute asks for six elements and lets the owner or someone authorized by the owner sign. It has to be accurate and made in good faith. If you're unsure whether something infringes, or a dispute is heading to court, that's the time for a lawyer.

Can someone else file for me?

Yes. A notice can be signed by a person authorized to act for the owner. With me, that's a signed one-page Copyright Enforcement Authorization: it's free and lets me file in your name. I never need your passwords or logins.

Does one notice take the content down everywhere?

No. A notice reaches only the provider you send it to. Every other copy needs its own notice, and Google needs its own filing to drop a page from its results.
